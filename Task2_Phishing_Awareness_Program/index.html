What is phishing?

Phishing is an attack where a criminal pretends to be someone you trust (a bank, your employer, a delivery company) to trick you into giving up passwords, card details or money, or into installing malware. It usually arrives by email, but also by SMS (smishing), phone call (vishing) and QR code (quishing).

Email phishing

Mass messages posing as a known brand.

Spear phishing

Targeted at one person, using details about their job or life.

Smishing and vishing

Fake SMS or calls, for example “your KYC has expired”.

Business email compromise

A fake message from a boss or vendor asking for a payment.

How to spot a suspicious email

Study this sample. The highlighted parts are the red flags.

From: Account Security <support@secure-paypa1-alerts.com>
Subject: URGENT: Your account will be closed in 24 hours
Dear Customer,

We detected unusual activity. Verify your identity immediately or your account will be permanently suspended.

Click here to verify: http://paypa1-verify.example/login

Attachment: invoice_2026.html.exe

Red flags checklist

  • Look-alike sender domain. “paypa1” uses the digit 1 instead of the letter l. Check the real domain after the @.
  • Urgency or threats. Pressure is designed to stop you thinking.
  • Generic greeting. Real companies usually use your name.
  • Mismatched link. Hover over a link (do not click) and compare the real address with the text.
  • Unexpected attachment. Double extensions like .html.exe, or macro-enabled Office files, are classic malware carriers.
  • Requests for secrets. No legitimate service asks for your password, PIN or OTP by email.

Fake login pages and fraudulent websites

Compare these addresses

https://accounts.google.com/signin

https://accounts.google.com.login-verify.net/signin

Read a web address from right to left up to the first single slash. The real domain is the part just before .com, .in and so on. In the second address the real domain is login-verify.net, not Google.

  • Typosquatting: amaz0n.com, micros0ft-support.com, sbi-online-kyc.co.
  • HTTPS is not proof of safety. Criminals can get padlocks too. It only means the connection is encrypted.
  • Check the basics: poor spelling, low-quality logos, pop-ups asking for card details, or pages reached from a message rather than typed by you.
  • Safer habit: open the site yourself from a bookmark or by typing the address, and use a password manager. It will not autofill on a look-alike domain.

Social engineering techniques

Phishing works because it targets people, not machines. Common levers:

Urgency

“Act in 24 hours.” Time pressure reduces careful thinking.

Authority

Pretending to be the CEO, police, the tax office or IT support.

Fear

“Your account is hacked” or “a warrant is issued”.

Greed and curiosity

Prizes, refunds, “see who viewed your profile”.

Pretexting

An invented story, such as a new vendor with changed bank details.

Baiting

A free USB drive or a free download that carries malware.

Practical tips to avoid phishing

  • Pause and verify. Contact the organisation through its official website or phone number, not the details in the message.
  • Turn on multi-factor authentication everywhere, preferably with an authenticator app or hardware key. Never share an OTP with anyone.
  • Use a password manager and a unique password for each account.
  • Keep software updated and use a reputable spam filter and antivirus.
  • Do not open unexpected attachments and be careful with QR codes in public places.
  • Report it. Tell your IT team, mark the mail as phishing, and in India report cyber fraud at cybercrime.gov.in or by calling 1930.
  • If you clicked, change the password immediately, enable MFA, scan the device and inform your bank or employer.

Case studies

RSA SecurID breach (2011)

Employees received a spear-phishing email with an Excel attachment titled around a recruitment plan. Opening it installed malware that led to the theft of information related to RSA’s SecurID products. Lesson: even security companies fall for well-targeted lures.

Google and Facebook invoice fraud (2013–2015)

A man impersonated a hardware vendor and sent fake invoices, with forged emails and documents, to staff at both companies, who paid over $100 million in total. Most of it was later recovered. Lesson: verify any change in payment details through a second channel.

Twitter account takeover (2020)

Attackers phoned Twitter employees pretending to be internal IT and directed them to a fake login page, gaining access to internal tools and then to high-profile accounts. Lesson: vishing plus a fake login page defeats trust in a familiar voice.

Everyday example: the KYC SMS

A text claims your bank account will be blocked unless you “update KYC” through a link, then the page asks for your card number and OTP. Lesson: banks do not ask for OTPs or card details through links.
